## Slide 3 — The headline finding

**VAP worked as a *shared vocabulary* — and it stayed with fellows after the canvases were gone.**

- Every fellow internalized the framework's vocabulary (values, tensions, dissolution / compromise / trade-off)
- Several kept using it to reason about their projects *without recalling the framework itself*
- We frame this as VAP delivering a **hermeneutical resource**: a way of *naming* what was already there

Speaker notes: This is our most consistent finding across all seven cases. The vocabulary persisted. Olzhas put it bluntly: he's now applying values reasoning in his personal life. Laura uses the grammar fluently throughout her interview. The framework's most reliable output isn't a particular design move — it's the practice fellows take into their next project.

---

## Slide 4 — VAP helps you *name* what was already there

> "In terms of values, I don't think we ever, like, have a discussion about values." — Andrea (JustFix)

> "I had always known translation evaluation was a complex and difficult task. I hadn't really thought of it as conflicting values." — Kiara (OTI)

> "I was feeling them in my soul, for the lack of a better word… they came up as concrete words during the framework." — Olzhas (CFS)

- Most of you already *sense* the tensions in your project.
- VAP gives you the words to **say** them — and once said, they're available for reasoning, defending in a meeting, and contesting with a stakeholder.

Speaker notes: This is what discovery looks like in practice — not learning new values from scratch, but making implicit commitments explicit. Andrea was "super aware" of the tension before VAP, but she'd never named it in a project conversation. After: she could.

---

## Slide 5 — Translation: holding a value steady, changing the words

> "Using the term *equity* would have been really loaded in these circles, so instead I talked about *rider performance*, and even though I was getting at the value of equity, that was terminology that was much less charged." — Laura (Riders Alliance)

> "The way I've been calling *recall* is *comprehensiveness*… *precision* is *high-quality predictions*." — Jennah (NYCEM)

- VAP's vocabulary often **disappears from view** in stakeholder conversations.
- What persists is the *translation move*: holding a value steady while changing the words that name it.
- You will do this work — across your host's culture, your collaborators, the public.

Speaker notes: This was one of the most generative patterns. Fellows didn't bring VAP's vocabulary into stakeholder meetings — they translated it. Jennah turned ML metrics into values language so non-technical teams could engage. Laura swapped politically loaded terms for de-charged equivalents to keep the conversation productive.

## Slide 6 — Three resolution strategies — real moves

**Dissolution** — redesign the problem so the conflict goes away
- *Hauke (YAI):* pose detection on optically blurred images + a rotating "blocker" carousel that makes the camera's state legible to the resident — *"both privacy AND functionality are achieved without compromise."*
- *Jennah (NYCEM):* a side-by-side map of the existing Heat Vulnerability Index vs. her ML risk score — let the stakeholder make the value-loaded choice at decision time.

**Compromise** — partial satisfaction of both values
- *Olzhas (CFS):* sensors over cameras + an alert protocol that notifies the resident first. *"As the domain of safety expands, your domain of dignity expands as well."*
- *Kiara (OTI):* a categorical *"Catastrophic"* tier for actively harmful errors, normal weighting elsewhere.

**Trade-off** — explicit prioritization, with the cost named
- *Andrea (JustFix):* keep the LLM, **change the use case** — redirect from legal-info to emotional support, where hallucination is lower-risk.
- *Erica (NYCPS):* short, factual recommendation lists (compromise) + equal-access prioritized over residual inclusion risk (trade-off).

Speaker notes: These are *examples of how fellows operationalized* the three canonical strategies — they didn't invent new categories, they put the categories to work in specific ways. Worth noting: Hauke's "Privacy Carousel" was the dissolution that came out of *fellow-introduced* privacy (no stakeholder had asked for privacy). That has its own implications — we'll come back to it.

## Slide 7 — Values as a complexity-reduction move

> "If you just debate over a feature… that can be, like, an infinite war… but if you can say that, oh, you know, but it kind of embodies this value, and this value is fundamentally not what we stand for… well, now you reduced complexity to a more powerful dimension." — Olzhas

- When a contested choice is framed as a **values conflict**, designers can decide which value takes priority — rather than stay stuck in irreducible empirical uncertainty about effects.
- Olzhas reached for the analogy of **religious conviction**: it anchors decisions that evidence alone cannot.

Speaker notes: One of the most striking things any fellow said. The mechanism: values reasoning compresses high-dimensional empirical debate into a tractable priority ordering. Especially useful when your team is stuck.

---

## Slide 8 — When does VAP *shape* design vs. just articulate it?

**Three conditions made the difference in 2025:**

1. **An open design space.** Where the brief was underspecified (Laura), VAP gave the project structure and changed what got built. Where the brief was foreclosed (Andrea — LLM + use case fixed before she arrived), VAP articulated but did not redirect.
2. **Sustained involvement** with the host organization — enough time for translation work to land in deliverables.
3. **Timing of introduction.** Six of seven fellows said VAP would have done more for them if it had arrived **earlier** in the project, before key decisions were foreclosed.

> "The framework, the framework itself did not lead to very transformational changes. I can imagine if it was a more vague project." — Olzhas

Speaker notes: You all have eight weeks. Two practical implications: (a) start the discovery work *early* — week 2, not week 6; (b) watch for the decisions your host has already made, and decide consciously where VAP can still shape vs. where it will mainly help you *name* what's happening.

## Slide 9 — A caution: VAP runs *in the designer's head*

- Both reflection and specification operate **within the designer's epistemic space**.
- In public-interest deployments, the **affected parties** — tenants, residents, students, riders, translation recipients — are precisely whose interests are at stake.
- They don't structurally enter VAP's loop. VAP names them as *objects* of discovery (you think *about* them), not as *participants* in the reflection (you think *with* them).

**Recommendation:** pair VAP with participatory methods. A more ambitious move: take the role of value *mediator* — surfacing and translating the values affected parties hold — rather than value *selector*.

Speaker notes: This is our most critical finding. It's not a study limitation, it's a structural property of the framework. The public-interest setting is what makes it visible — and what makes it matter. Several of you (Daniel, Tan, others) are working with populations whose voices are exactly the ones VAP doesn't structurally require. Worth thinking about from day one.

## Slide 10 — For *you*, this summer

- **Name your own values.** Hauke introduced *privacy* to his project — no stakeholder had asked for it. That counts. Use the "PiTech Fellow" prompt on the Discovery canvas.
- **Notice logistical decisions that are values decisions.** Erica: "as we were working through it just felt like, oh… things to navigate. Reflecting on, oh, what values were we choosing to push through."
- **Translate, don't transplant.** Your host's vocabulary > VAP's vocabulary in stakeholder rooms.
- **Be sceptical of the framework reaching the artifact.** A great write-up is not the same as a different system. Both are wins — but they're different wins.

Speaker notes: Close the loop. Then transition to the Discovery activity.
